# Granny hexagon cardigan

<a href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=vmsDH1WnDlA"><img src="https://img.youtube.com/vi/vmsDH1WnDlA/maxresdefault.jpg" alt="A folded teal granny hexagon cardigan, and the designer wearing a long rainbow-striped one" width="480"></a>

*The video's thumbnail; click to watch.*

A cardigan made from two big granny hexagons. Each hexagon is folded in
half and two of its sides are crocheted together as you go, which turns it
into a sleeve, a front and half a back in one piece, with no sewing. The two
pieces are joined at the centre back with a few rows of granny stripe, then
the sleeves and body are lengthened and the edges finished with ribbed cuffs
and a single-crochet border. It fits any size, because you measure the
hexagon against a sleeve that fits you. Intermediate: nothing harder than
double and single crochet, but it is a big, slow project, the fold and the
join-as-you-go seam take some visualising, and the sizing is on you.

## Materials

- Light/DK-weight yarn. The video gives no amounts; the designer made a
  cropped version and still had a whole cake left, which she used to add
  length. (Ours, unverified) Budget about 1,500–2,000 m (1,650–2,200 yd),
  that's 3–4 cakes of Horizon, for a medium with long sleeves and a
  mid-length body; more for larger sizes. Buy one extra from the same dye
  lot, see [yarn amounts](../../knowledge/techniques/garments.md#yarn-amounts).
- Hooks: 6.5 mm, 5.5 mm and 4 mm. Any yarn and hook work; the hexagon just
  takes more or fewer rounds.
- Tapestry needle, scissors
- Tape measure, and a hoodie or sweater whose sleeves fit the way you like

## Gauge

Not critical. The hexagon is worked until one side is as long as you want
the sleeve to measure around, so any yarn and hook will do; they only change
how many rounds that takes. The designer uses a hook two sizes bigger than
the yarn label suggests, for a looser, drapier fabric that also works up
faster.

## Before you start

### How two hexagons become a cardigan

Each hexagon is folded in half along a line through the middle of two
opposite sides. Number the sides 1–6 in the direction you crochet, side 1
starting at the corner where the rounds join. The last round is only partial:
it covers sides 1 to 4 and stops at the corner at the end of side 4.

```
                   stop here
                       P
                     /   \
            side 4 /       \ side 5
                 /           \
               N               Q
               |               |
        side 3 |  - - fold - - | side 6
               |               |
               M               R   start of the round
                 \           /
            side 2 \       / side 1
                     \   /
                       A
```

Fold the lower half up behind the upper half, so the stop corner lands on the
start corner. Now:

- **Side 6** is folded in half on itself. Its two halves make an opening:
  the **sleeve**. Its full length is the sleeve's circumference, which is
  what you size the hexagon by.
- **Side 5** lies on **side 1**. These two get crocheted together, from the
  neck point down to the sleeve opening: the seam runs along the **top of the
  sleeve**, over the shoulder.
- **Side 4** and **side 2** stay open, one in front of the other. One is the
  **centre-front edge**, the other the **centre-back edge**. Both run from
  the neck point down to the hem.
- **Side 3** is folded in half on itself: the **hem**, half for the front,
  half for the back.
- The fold is the side of the body and the underside of the sleeve. The
  hexagon's centre ends up at the underarm.

```
                    neck point (stop corner over start corner)
                          /\
      front edge        /    \      top of the sleeve:
      (side 4, with   /        \    side 5 crocheted
      side 2, the   /            \  to side 1
      back edge,  /                \
      behind it) |                  | sleeve opening
             hem |                  | (side 6, folded)
      (side 3,   |__________________|
       folded)      fold: side of the body and underarm
```

Make two. Lay them out as a cardigan, neck points together, and the two edges
that meet in the middle are the back edges. A strip of granny-stripe rows is
added to each back edge and the strips are joined, which gives the neck its
room. Then rows are added along the hem for length, the sleeves get rounds
and cuffs, and a border goes round the whole outer edge.

(Ours) One measurement sets everything, because a hexagon's sides are all the
same: sleeve circumference, the length from the neck point down the front
edge to the hem, and the sleeve seam from the neck to the cuff are all one
side. The body measures two sides plus the back strip around the hem: about
102 cm (40 in) for the designer's 46 cm sides and 10 cm strip. Granny stitch
stretches, but if you want the body wider than your sleeve measurement gives,
the place to add width is the back strip.

### Granny hexagon conventions

- **A cluster** is 3 dc in the same space. You work into **spaces** (the
  gaps between the clusters of the round below), not into stitches.
- **Corners** are ch-1 spaces and get (cluster, ch 1, cluster). **Side
  spaces** get 1 cluster. There is **no chain between clusters** along the
  sides; the next round's cluster goes into the gap between two clusters.
- The designer chains 1 at corners. Ch 2 if you prefer; just be consistent.
- **Every round starts in a corner**: ch 3 counts as the first dc of that
  corner's first cluster, and the round ends with the same corner's second
  cluster, ch 1, and a sl st into the top of the ch 3. Starting at a corner
  rather than mid-side matters here: the partial round and the fold depend on
  it.
- Don't turn between rounds. The rows added later (back strip, body) are
  turned, and start with **ch 2 counting as a dc**: the designer uses ch 2
  rather than ch 3 to keep those edges tight.

### Join-as-you-go seam

Two edges are joined by crocheting one and slip stitching into the other, in
the same pass. With the edges folded together, one faces you (the front) and
one is behind (the back). Make a cluster into the next space on the front,
through that layer only. Then push the hook into the next space on the back,
yarn over, pull through the space and through the loop on the hook: a slip
stitch that pins the layers together. Cluster, slip stitch, cluster, slip
stitch, to the end. The seam starts and ends with a slip stitch. Both faces
of granny stitch look alike, so there is no wrong side to worry about.

### Ribbed cuff attached as it's made

The cuffs are strips of sc ribbing (rows in back loops only) worked
sideways, at right angles to the sleeve. Every second row ends at the sleeve,
where its last stitch is worked together with the next stitch of the sleeve
edge and then a slip stitch into the stitch after that. So the strip grows
along the sleeve edge, and when the last sleeve stitch is used up, the two
ends of the strip are slip stitched together.

## Hexagon (make 2)

**Start:** make a magic ring.

**R1:** ch 3 (counts as dc), 2 dc into the ring, ch 1; [3 dc, ch 1] 5 times.
Pull the tail to close the ring. Sl st into the top of the ch 3.
— 6 clusters, 6 corner spaces (18 dc)

**R2:** ch 3, 2 dc into the corner space just behind the hook (the last
ch 1 of R1). In each of the next 5 corner spaces: 3 dc, ch 1, 3 dc. Back in
the first corner: 3 dc, ch 1, sl st into the top of the ch 3.
— 12 clusters, 2 in each corner (36 dc)

**R3:** ch 3, 2 dc into the corner space behind the hook. \*3 dc in the gap
between the next two clusters (a side space); 3 dc, ch 1, 3 dc in the next
corner space\*; repeat from \* 4 more times. 3 dc in the last side space.
Back in the first corner: 3 dc, ch 1, sl st into the top of the ch 3.
— 18 clusters, 1 on each side between the corners (54 dc)

**R4:** the same, with 2 side spaces on each side. — 24 clusters (72 dc)

**R5 onward:** every round is the same, with one more side space per side
than the round before. Round n has 6n clusters (18n dc), n − 2 of them on
each side between the corners.

### When to stop: sizing

Lay a hoodie or sweater whose sleeves you like flat. Measure the sleeve
straight across, just below the armhole, not along the slanted armhole seam.
Double it: a sleeve has two sides. The designer measured 21.5–23 cm
(8.5–9 in), aimed for 43 cm (17 in), and stopped when **one side of her
hexagon, corner to corner, measured 46 cm (18 in)**. Keep adding rounds until
one side is as long as the sleeve circumference you want.

Measure every round or two near the end. The written pattern gives round
counts per size; from the video, it's the tape measure.

## Last round, fold and seam

**Partial round:** ch 3, 2 dc into the corner space behind the hook. Work
sides 1, 2, 3 and 4 as usual: 1 cluster in every side space, (3 dc, ch 1,
3 dc) in the three corners between them. At the corner at the end of side 4,
put **only one cluster**: no ch 1, no second cluster. Stop here. Sides 5 and 6
stay unworked. — four sides' worth: 4(n + 1) clusters for a hexagon of
n full rounds (Ours)

**Fold** the hexagon in half as described above, so the corner where the
hook is lands on the corner where this round started (the one with the
ch 3). Side 5, unworked, lies on side 1, worked at the start of this round.
Side 6 folds on itself and makes the sleeve opening.

**Seam** (front = side 5, back = side 1):

1. Sl st into the corner space on the **back**, right under the hook: the
   corner at the start of side 2.
2. 3 dc into the corner space on the **front**, through that layer only:
   this is the second cluster of the corner you stopped at, with the sl st
   standing in for the ch 1.
3. Sl st into the next space along the back (side 1). 3 dc into the next
   space along the front (side 5). Repeat down the sides.
4. The last cluster goes into the corner space at the far end of side 5.
   Then sl st into the **top of the ch 3** at the start of the round, which
   lies behind it. Done: the seam runs from the neck point to the sleeve
   opening, and the yarn is at the sleeve opening.

— n + 1 clusters on the front, n + 2 slip stitches into the back, for a
hexagon of n full rounds (Ours)

Try it on. The piece is now a short sleeve with a front and half a back
hanging off it.

## Sleeve

Rounds of clusters around the sleeve opening, no turning.

**Sleeve R1:** ch 3 (counts as dc), 2 dc in the space between the two
clusters the last sl st joined. Then 3 dc in every space around the opening:
the gaps between clusters along side 6, and the step on each side where the
corner clusters of the partial round meet them. Sl st into the top of the
ch 3. — n + 2 clusters for a hexagon of n full rounds (Ours; count the
spaces on this round and keep that number)

**Sleeve R2 onward:** ch 3, 2 dc in the gap behind the hook, 3 dc in every
gap around. Sl st into the top of the ch 3. — same count as R1

Repeat until the sleeve reaches the top of your wrist (try it on as you go),
or shorter if you like. The designer worked hers to the wrist and added
cuffs; without a cuff the sleeve stays wide and open, which she also likes.

## Cuff

**Decrease round** (same 6.5 mm hook): [1 sc in the next st, sc2tog over
the next 2 sts] around; each cluster is 3 sts, so that's one repeat per
cluster. Sl st into the first sc. — 2 sc per cluster, so 2(n + 2) sc (Ours)

**Ribbing**, with the 4 mm hook. A smaller hook makes tighter, better-looking
ribbing; the designer goes down two or three sizes. No turning chains.

**Row 1:** ch 11, or as many as you want the cuff deep. Sc in the 2nd ch
from the hook and in each ch along. Make the last sc also catch the sleeve:
insert in the last ch, yarn over and pull up a loop, insert in the next sc of
the decrease round, yarn over and pull up a loop, yarn over and pull through
all the loops. Sl st into the next sc of the sleeve. Turn. — 10 sc

**Row 2** (away from the sleeve): sc in the first st through both loops,
sc in back loops only in each st across, sc in the last st through both
loops. Turn. — 10 sc

**Row 3** (toward the sleeve): sc in the first st through both loops, sc in
back loops only across, working the last sc together with the next free sc
of the sleeve as in row 1. Sl st into the next sleeve sc. Turn. — 10 sc

Repeat rows 2–3 until every sc of the decrease round has been used; it
comes out an even number of rows, ending with a row 2.

**Close the cuff:** fold the strip so the last row lies against the starting
chain. Sl st through both, one st of the last row and the matching chain at a
time, from the outer edge to the sleeve. One more sl st at the sleeve edge,
cut, pull the tail through and weave it in.

Then make the second hexagon, seam, sleeve and cuff **exactly the same**:
write down the round count, the number of sleeve rounds and the number of
ribbing rows.

## Back and neck

Lay the two pieces out as a cardigan, neck points together, sleeves pointing
out. The two straight edges that meet in the middle are the back edges; mark
them. (The designer once added the strip to a front edge by mistake.)

Each back edge gets a strip of **3 rows** of granny stripe, about 5 cm (2 in)
each, so the back neck gets about 10 cm (4 in) of room. In a thicker yarn you
need fewer rows; for a wider neck or body, more (Ours).

### Strip on the first piece (6.5 mm hook)

**Row 1:** slip knot on the hook. Starting at the neck point: 1 dc in the
corner space at the top of the back edge. Then 3 dc in each space down the
edge, ending with 3 dc in the corner space at the hem. Turn.
— 1 dc + k clusters, where k is the number of spaces down the edge
(n + 1 for a hexagon of n full rounds (Ours))

**Row 2:** ch 2 (counts as dc), 3 dc in each gap between row 1's clusters,
and last of all 3 dc into the **top of row 1's lone dc**; the designer puts
it in the stitch, not the space beside it, because it holds better. Turn.
— 1 dc + k clusters

**Row 3:** ch 2, 3 dc in each gap between row 2's clusters, the last one in
the gap beside row 2's ch 2. Fasten off. — 1 dc + k clusters

### Strip on the second piece, joined

Rows 1 and 2 the same. Row 3 is a join-as-you-go seam to the first strip's
row 3: lay the pieces out again with the two strips side by side, edge to
edge.

**Row 3 (joining):** ch 2. Sl st into the top of the first strip's row 3
where it begins (its chain). 2 dc in the first gap on this strip, so this
side has a cluster where the other does. \*Sl st into the next space on the
other strip, 3 dc in the next gap on this strip\*; repeat to the hem. The
other strip ends in a single dc, so match it: after the last cluster and its
sl st, 1 dc in the last gap, then sl st into the end of the other strip's
row. Fasten off. (Ours, unverified: the start and end of this row are our
best reading of the video; the principle, cluster for cluster with a slip
stitch into the other strip after each, is clear.)

Nothing is sewn: the cardigan now hangs together at the back.

## Body length (optional)

For a cropped cardigan, skip to the border. Otherwise rows of granny stripe
go along the hem, the full width from one front corner to the other. They
are rows, not rounds: the cardigan is open at the front.

**Row 1:** turn the cardigan upside down. Slip knot on the hook; join at the
bottom corner of one front edge with 3 dc in that corner space. Then 3 dc in
each space along the hem: across the front, across the back of that piece,
then across the bottom of the back strips (a cluster at the end of each
strip's rows and one in the middle where they were joined), then across the
other piece, ending with 3 dc in its front corner space. Turn.
— k clusters, where k is the number of spaces along the hem; count them

**Row 2:** ch 2 (counts as dc), 3 dc in each gap between row 1's clusters,
then 1 dc into the first dc of row 1's first cluster, so both ends of the row
finish with a lone dc. Turn. — (k − 1) clusters + 2 dc

**Row 3:** ch 2, 2 dc in the first gap (the ch 2 and 2 dc are the first
cluster), 3 dc in each gap between row 2's clusters, the last one in the gap
beside row 2's ch 2. Turn. — k clusters

Repeat rows 2–3 until the body is the length you want. End with a row 3 (a
cluster row); the border is easier to work along it. Fasten off.

## Border

Switch to the 5.5 mm hook: one size down makes a firmer edge. Five rounds of
sc round the whole outer edge: up one front, across the back neck, down the
other front, along the hem. Only the two bottom front corners are corners;
the neck is worked straight.

**Round 1:** join at the bottom corner of one front edge: 1 sc in the corner
stitch. Up the front edge:

- along the body rows, 1 sc into the top of each row-end dc and 1 sc around
  its post, so 2 per row;
- along the hexagon's edge, where the stitch tops face you, 1 sc in each
  stitch.

Across the top of the back strips: roughly 1 sc per row end or space, as
evenly as you can; it's a short stretch. Down the other front edge the same
way. At its bottom corner: 1 sc, ch 1, 1 sc in the corner stitch. Along the
hem, 1 sc in each dc. Back at the start: 1 sc in the same stitch as the first
sc, ch 1, sl st into the first sc, so that corner matches the other.
— as many sc as there are stitches and row ends; the count isn't needed

**Rounds 2–5:** sc in the sc you slip stitched into, then 1 sc in each sc
around; in each corner ch-1 space: 1 sc, ch 1, 1 sc. At the end, sl st into
the first sc. — 2 more sc than the round before

**Last round:** after the last sc, don't slip stitch. Cut the yarn with a
long tail and pull it through. Thread the tail, pass the needle under both
top loops of the round's first sc, then back down through the top of the last
sc: an invisible join that makes the top look continuous.

## Finishing

Weave in all the ends. There are a lot at the back strips and body rows: the
designer ignores them while working and does them all at the end.

(Ours) Acrylic doesn't need blocking; a light steam if the border curls, see
[blocking a garment](../../knowledge/techniques/garments.md#blocking-a-garment).

## Tips from the source

- **Any yarn, any hook.** Only the number of rounds changes. She goes up two
  hook sizes from the label for drape and speed.
- **Start every round at a corner.** It's what makes the partial round, the
  fold and the no-sew seam line up.
- **Try it on once the seam is done.** The sleeve is closed up to the
  shoulder, so you can check the sleeve length on your arm before each round.
- **Make the second piece identical.** Count rounds, sleeve rounds and
  ribbing rows.
- **Mark the back edge** before adding the strip.
- **Smaller hooks for cuffs and border**: two or three sizes down for the
  ribbing, one down for the border, especially with a thin yarn and a loose
  main fabric.
- **End the body on a cluster row** before the border.
- **Leave the ends** and weave them all in at the end.

## Variations

- **Cropped:** skip the body rows; the border goes straight on.
- **Short sleeves:** stop after the seam, or after a few sleeve rounds.
- **No cuffs:** leave the sleeves wide and open.
- The designer's written pattern is graded S to XXXL, with round counts per
  size, for those who'd rather not measure.
- (Ours, untested) **Stripes:** a self-striping cake does the colour work
  for you. With solid colours, change at the join of a round; keep the partial
  round, the seam and the first sleeve round in one colour so the seam
  doesn't show.
- (Ours, untested) **Wider body:** make the back strip more rows; it adds
  width to the body and the neck without touching the sleeves.
