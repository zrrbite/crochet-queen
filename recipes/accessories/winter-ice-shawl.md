# Winter Ice shawl

<a href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=9fZ_-BVROBg"><img src="https://img.youtube.com/vi/9fZ_-BVROBg/maxresdefault.jpg" alt="A blue-green lace shawl with a tassel at each of its three corners, draped round the neck of a dress form" width="480"></a>

*The video's thumbnail; click to watch.*

A triangular lace shawl from one skein of fingering-weight yarn. It starts at
the bottom point with a magic ring and grows a shell wider every two rows
until it's a wide, shallow triangle to wrap round your shoulders. The lace is
a two-row repeat of shells (three small clusters with chains between) and
V-stitches, worked with a hook well above the yarn's size so it drapes. The
designer calls it easy, and the repeat itself is: once rows 1–7 are in, every
row is the same few moves. We rate it **experienced** because the yarn is
fine and the hook is loose, so the tension is hard to keep even; because
every shell, sc and V has one right landing place and the rows run to 50-odd
groups near the top with no safe place to miscount; and because the finished
piece is only as good as its blocking, which here is a wet soak and a careful
pinning out, not an optional extra.

## Materials

- One skein of fingering-weight yarn, 400–425 m. Any fingering yarn works;
  a hand-dyed sock yarn or a gradient cake shows the lace well. A bigger cake
  gives a bigger shawl.
- 4.5 mm hook, or whatever gives you a loose, open tension in your yarn.
- A little contrast yarn for the tassels (optional).
- Tapestry needle, scissors.
- For blocking: rust-proof pins and something to pin into (foam blocking
  mats, a yoga mat, or a towel over a spare bed). Blocking wires help with
  the long top edge but aren't needed. (Ours)
- A stitch marker or two. (Ours)

## Gauge

Not critical: the designer gives none. What matters is drape, so she uses a
hook one size larger than the yarn's label recommends, and 4.5 mm on sock
yarn is bigger than that. If your first few rows feel stiff, go up a hook
size; the lace should hang, not stand. A small
[swatch](../../knowledge/techniques/garments.md#gauge-swatch) of rows 1–7
tells you quickly, and doubles as practice for the repeat.

## Before you start

**The shape.** The shawl is worked flat in rows, bottom-up. Row 1 is the
point at the bottom; every row after it is a little longer than the last, and
the long top edge is the final row. Each pair of rows adds one shell to the
width, so the triangle is wide and shallow: the designer's is well over
twice as wide as it is deep.

**The two kinds of row.** After the setup, rows alternate:

- **V rows (odd rows)** start with ch 6 and end with a treble. Across the
  row you put a V-stitch into every sc and an sc into the top of every shell,
  with ch 2 between everything.
- **Shell rows (even rows)** start and end with an sc. Across the row you put
  an sc into every sc and a shell into every V-stitch, with ch 1 between
  everything. The first sc goes into the top of the previous row's treble,
  the last into its starting ch 6.

**How it grows.** A V row has a treble standing at each end, outside the
V-stitches. The shell row puts an sc into each treble, and the V row after
that puts a V-stitch into each of those sc. So every V row has one more
V-stitch than the one before, every shell row one more shell. That is the
whole increase; there's nothing else to remember at the edges.

**Special stitches**, as the designer defines them:

- **2-dc cluster (2 dc cl):** yo, insert the hook in the space, yo, pull up a
  loop, yo, pull through 2 loops (2 loops left on the hook). Yo, insert the
  hook in the *same* space, yo, pull up a loop, yo, pull through 2 loops (3
  loops on the hook). Yo and pull through all 3. It's a dc2tog with both
  legs in one place: a little pointed stitch.
- **V-stitch (V-st):** 1 dc, ch 1, 1 dc, all in the stitch named.
- **Shell:** 2 dc cl, ch 2, 2 dc cl, ch 2, 2 dc cl, all worked into the ch-1
  space in the middle of a V-stitch. Three small points with two chain gaps
  between them, not the fan of plain dc that "shell" usually means.

**Where stitches land.**

- *sc in the centre of a shell* goes into the top of the shell's middle
  cluster (the loop that closed it), not into a ch-2 space.
- *V-st in next sc* goes into the sc stitch itself.
- *1st st* of a V row is the sc the ch 6 rises from: the V-stitch shares it.
  *Last st* of a V row is the final sc of the row below, and it takes the
  V-stitch, the ch 2 **and** the treble.
- *4th ch of the beginning ch 6:* count up from the base of the chain. Chains
  1–4 stand in for a treble and chains 5–6 are the ch-2 space, so the 4th
  chain is the "top of the treble", and it's where the last sc of every shell
  row goes. Put a stitch marker in it as you make each ch 6, and the end of
  the next row finds itself. (Ours)

**Chains.** The ch 6 at the start of a V row **counts** as 1 tr + ch 2. The
ch 1 at the start of a shell row **does not count**. Turn after every row.

## Pattern

Make a magic ring.

**R1:** ch 6 (counts as tr + ch 2). Into the ring: V-st, ch 2, 1 tr. Turn,
then pull the ring tight. — 2 tr, 1 V-st, 2 ch-2 sps

**R2:** ch 1, 1 sc in the first st (the top of the tr), ch 1, shell in the
V-st, ch 1, 1 sc in the 4th ch of the beginning ch 6. Turn.
— 1 shell, 2 sc, 2 ch-1 sps

**R3:** ch 6, V-st in the first st (the sc under the ch 6), ch 2, 1 sc in the
centre of the shell, ch 2, [V-st, ch 2, 1 tr] in the last st. Turn.
— 2 tr, 2 V-st, 1 sc, 4 ch-2 sps

**R4:** ch 1, 1 sc in the first st, ch 1, shell in the next V-st, ch 1, 1 sc
in the next sc, ch 1, shell in the next V-st, ch 1, 1 sc in the 4th ch of the
beginning ch 6. Turn. — 2 shells, 3 sc, 4 ch-1 sps

**R5:** ch 6, V-st in the first st, ch 2, sc in the centre of the next shell,
ch 2, V-st in the next sc, ch 2, sc in the centre of the next shell, ch 2,
[V-st, ch 2, 1 tr] in the last st. Turn. — 2 tr, 3 V-st, 2 sc, 6 ch-2 sps

**R6 (shell row):** ch 1, 1 sc in the first st, \*ch 1, shell in the next
V-st, ch 1, 1 sc in the next sc\*; rep from \* to \* across, making the last
sc in the 4th ch of the beginning ch 6. Turn. — 3 shells, 4 sc, 6 ch-1 sps

**R7 (V row):** ch 6, V-st in the first st, \*ch 2, sc in the centre of the
next shell, ch 2, V-st in the next sc\*; rep from \* to \* across, ending the
last repeat with [V-st, ch 2, 1 tr] in the last st. Turn.
— 2 tr, 4 V-st, 3 sc, 8 ch-2 sps

The counts for R6 and R7 are ours; the pattern gives counts for rows 1–5
only. The rule: a shell row has one shell per V-stitch below it and one sc
more than shells; a V row has one V-stitch per sc below it, one sc per shell,
and two trebles. (Ours)

**Rows 6 and 7 are the repeat.** Repeat them, R6 then R7, until the shawl is
as big as you want. The designer's shawl is **54 rows**: rows 8–54 are 24
more pairs, ending on a shell row with 27 shells and 28 sc across the top.

**When to stop.**

- Stop after a **shell row** (an even row), so the top edge is a row of
  shells, not a row of loose V-stitches and trebles. The designer's R54 is a
  shell row.
- The designer's rule is simply: stop when it's big enough. One skein of
  400–425 m gives her size; more yarn, more rows.
- Measure with the shawl stretched gently: it grows a lot in blocking, so
  the unblocked piece is a poor guide. (Ours)
- The rows get longer as you go, so the yarn goes faster and faster. The
  last pair of rows of the designer's 54 takes about a fourteenth of the
  skein: roughly 30 m, or 7 g of a 100 g skein. Weigh what's left before
  starting a V row, and if there's less than about 10% of the skein, stop
  after the shell row you're on. (Ours, estimate)

**Fasten off** after your last shell row: cut the yarn, leaving a tail, and
pull it through.

## Edging

There isn't one. The pattern ends with the last shell row and fastens off:
the shells give the top edge a soft scallop, and the sides are the trebles
and sc of the row ends, which block out into a neat line. The tassels, if
you add them, go on the three corners. If you want something more, a row of
sc along the top edge would be the obvious addition, but it's not in the
pattern and we haven't tried it. (Ours, untested)

## Finishing

Blocking is what turns a crumpled triangle into a lace shawl. The designer
says it makes a huge difference and not to skip it; the stitches only open up
once the shawl has been wet and pinned.

1. **Weave in all ends first**, including the magic-ring tail at the bottom
   point. Ends woven in after blocking pull the lace out of shape. (Ours)
2. **Soak** the shawl in lukewarm water until it's wet through. A drop of
   wool wash is fine but not needed. (Ours) Lift it out supporting the
   whole weight: wet lace stretches.
3. **Wring gently in a towel:** lay the shawl on a bath towel, roll the
   towel up and press to take most of the water out. Don't twist it.
4. **Lay it out** on your blocking surface, pull it gently into the triangle,
   and bring it out to the finished size: the designer's measured 168 cm
   across the top and 63.5 cm from top edge to point.
5. **Pin.** The designer's advice is to take your time here, because this
   step decides how the finished piece looks. A way that works (Ours): pin
   the top edge first, as a straight line, with the two top corners set the
   full width apart; then pin the bottom point straight down from the
   centre; then work along each side, pinning through the row-end loops
   every few rows so the edge runs straight and the shells open evenly. Pin
   the top edge through the ch-1 spaces between shells, or thread a blocking
   wire through them before pinning.
6. **Leave it to dry completely** before taking a single pin out. Overnight
   is usual. (Ours)
7. **Tassels (optional).** The designer puts one on each of the three
   corners, in a slightly darker shade of the same colour. For each, wrap
   yarn about 30 times round a book 15–20 cm wide, with a 30 cm length of
   yarn laid across the top of the book first. Knot that length tightly
   round the top of the bundle, cut the bundle open at the bottom, wind
   another length round the tassel about 2.5 cm below the top five or so
   times and knot it. Trim the ends level and sew the tassel securely to a
   corner.

## Tips from the source

- **Concentrate until the increases are established.** The stitches are all
  basic, but rows 1–7 need attention; after that the rhythm takes over.
- **Look at the shape after row 4.** The designer points out the triangle
  already forming; if yours isn't widening at both ends, check that the end
  sc of each shell row went into the 4th chain of the ch 6 and that the end
  of each V row has both a V-stitch and a treble in the last sc.
- **Pull the magic ring tight** only after row 1 is done and you've turned;
  weave in that tail at the end.
- **A bigger hook than the label says** is the point, not a mistake: it's
  what gives the lace its drape. A 4.5 mm on sock yarn feels odd for the
  first row or two.
- **Where the sc goes on a shell:** into the top of the middle cluster, the
  tightest point of the shell, not the chain gap beside it.
- **Count in groups, not stitches** (Ours): at the end of each row, count
  shells (or V-stitches). Each V row should have one more V than the last V
  row, each shell row one more shell. That catches a skipped V-stitch long
  before it shows.
- **Gradient yarn** looks good in this stitch, the designer says; a larger
  gradient cake just makes a larger shawl, and you can stop whenever it's
  big enough. The lace "blooms" after blocking.

## Variations

- **Size:** more or fewer repeats of rows 6–7. Fewer rows makes a kerchief
  or head scarf (one of the designer's readers did this as a test); more yarn
  makes a wrap. The yarn needed changes with the size; see
  [yarn amounts](../../knowledge/techniques/garments.md#yarn-amounts).
- **Heavier yarn:** the designer says DK or light worsted with a matching
  hook works beautifully, for a warmer, bigger shawl. (More yarn needed.)
- **Scarf:** asked for a scarf version, the designer said the stitch pattern
  would make a lovely one. Working rows of a fixed width instead of
  increasing is the obvious way, but no pattern exists for it. (Ours,
  untested)
- **No tassels**, or tassels in the main yarn instead of a contrast.
