# Tunisian crochet

Crochet with a long hook that holds a whole row of loops at once. Each row is
made in two passes: a **forward pass** that picks up a loop from every stitch
and keeps them all on the hook, like casting on in knitting, and a **return
pass** that works them off again two at a time. The work is never turned, so
the right side always faces you. The fabric sits somewhere between knitting
and crochet: denser and squarer than ordinary crochet, with a grid of
vertical bars on the front and ridges on the back.

The first row is the hardest part. Once it's on the hook, the rest is
repetitive and quick.

## The hook

- **A plain crochet hook** is fine for learning, as long as the shaft is
  straight and bare: no ergonomic handle, no big thumb rest. The loops pile
  up along the shaft, and anything on it gets in the way. Width is limited to
  what fits on the shaft, so this is for swatches and narrow things.
- **A straight Tunisian hook** is longer, with a hook at one end and a
  stopper at the other so loops can't slide off the back. Your piece can only
  be as wide as the hook: scarves, at a push sweater panels.
- **A corded hook** has a flexible cable and a stopper behind the hook head.
  The longer the cord, the wider the work: sweaters, shawls, blankets. Sets
  come with several hook sizes, cords and stoppers; the guide recommends
  Clover's set for beginners, because the cords attach without tools and it's
  cheap. For a garment, a cord of at least 60 cm (24 in). One trick from the
  cardigan video: use one long cord without a stopper and tuck its end under
  your thigh or between the sofa cushions, so it doesn't flop around.
- **A pointed hook head** makes it much easier to find the back bumps of the
  chain and the spaces between stitches.
- **Go up a size or two** from what you'd use for the same yarn in ordinary
  crochet: about 6 mm for DK, 6.5–8 mm for worsted. The bigger hook keeps the
  loops loose, which matters (see [curling](#curling-and-tension)).

## Yarn

Learn with DK or worsted weight. Acrylic or wool have enough slip on a metal,
wood or plastic hook to keep your tension even; cotton works but is harder to
keep even at first. A smooth, light-coloured yarn makes the bars easy to see.
See [yarn and hooks](../materials/yarn-and-hooks.md).

## Holding the hook and the work

- Hold the hook as you normally would. The loops gather along the shaft
  behind the head; before each return pass, push them down towards the head
  so they're easy to work off.
- The work is **never turned**. The forward pass goes right to left across
  the row and the return pass works back left to right, both with the right
  side facing you. (Left-handers mirror everything. Ours.)
- When you need to see the edge stitch, pinch the edge of the work between
  finger and thumb and tilt it towards you.
- Keep everything **loose**: the chain, the loops you pull up, the return
  pass, the bind-off. Tight Tunisian crochet collapses and curls, and the hook
  won't go under the bars on the next row.

## Forward pass and return pass

Every row has the same two halves, whatever stitch it uses.

**Forward pass:** working right to left, pull up a loop in each stitch and
leave it on the hook. The loop already on the hook at the start **counts as
the first stitch**. At the end you should have as many loops on the hook as
the row has stitches; count them now, before the return pass. This is the
cheapest place to find a mistake.

**Return pass:** working the loops off, left to right:

1. Yarn over and pull through **one** loop. This is the row's ch 1; it gives
   the row its height, like a turning chain in ordinary crochet.
2. Yarn over and pull through **two** loops. Repeat until one loop is left.

The return pass makes the row of horizontal bars that runs along the top of
each row. The one loop left on the hook is the first stitch of the next row.

## The foundation row

1. Make a [slip knot](../stitches/basic-stitches.md#slip-knot) and
   [chain](../stitches/basic-stitches.md#chain-ch) the number of stitches
   you want, **loosely**: you have to get the hook back into every chain.
2. Turn the chain over to find the **back bumps**, the row of little bumps
   along the back, between the two strands of the V. Working into these
   leaves both strands of the V free along the bottom edge, which gives a neat
   braid to add a border or ribbing to later.
3. Skip the first chain (the loop on the hook doesn't count as a chain).
   Insert the hook under the back bump of the **second chain from the hook**,
   yarn over, and pull up a loop. Leave it on the hook.
4. Repeat in every chain to the end. You should have as many loops as you
   made chains, counting the loop that was already on the hook.
5. Return pass.

That's the foundation row: a row of V's along the bottom, vertical bars above
them, and the return pass's horizontal bars along the top. Patterns usually
count it as row 1.

## Tunisian simple stitch (Tss)

The basic stitch, like sc in crochet or the knit stitch in knitting.

Looking at the row, each stitch has a **front vertical bar** and a back one
behind it. Tss is worked under the front bar only.

1. The loop on the hook counts as the first stitch, so **skip the first
   vertical bar**, the one directly under that loop at the right edge. Every
   Tunisian pattern assumes this; it isn't written out.
2. Insert the hook **right to left under the front vertical bar** of the next
   stitch, yarn over, and pull up a loop. Keep it loose and as tall as its
   neighbours.
3. Repeat across to the last stitch, which is worked differently (next
   section).
4. Count the loops, then return pass.

**Which row am I working into?** The row you're working has a small gap
between its vertical bars. The row below is filled in behind, so its bars
don't show the same gap. Don't touch those.

## The last stitch of the row (Lts)

The last stitch has **two strands** to catch, not one: its front vertical bar
and a shorter loop sitting just behind it, about half its height. Toni Lipsey
calls it the "shadow". Pinch the edge and tilt the work towards you to see
it. Insert the hook under **both**, yarn over, and pull up a loop as for Tss.
Catching both is what gives the left edge a firm, straight line; catching
only the front bar leaves the edge loose and loopy.

It's easy to mistake the top of the last stitch (where the return chain runs)
for its side. Pull the last stitch up and away from the rest of the row: the
top and the side separate, and the side is where the hook goes.

Patterns write this as **Lts** (last Tunisian stitch) or "edge stitch", and
some don't mention it at all; do it anyway.

## Tunisian knit stitch (Tks)

Insert the hook **between the front and back vertical bars** of the stitch,
front to back, so it comes out the back of the fabric; yarn over, pull up a
loop. Looks like a knitted stitch from the front. The
[Savannah cardi](../../recipes/clothing/savannah-cardi.md) uses it to work
into the hole a yarn over left on the row before: "tks the yo" means go down
through that hole and pull up a loop.

## Tunisian full stitch (Tfs)

Insert the hook into the **space between two stitches**, front to back
(between the back bar of one stitch and the front bar of the next); yarn
over, pull up a loop. Worked in the first space of a row and in the space
just before the last stitch, it adds a stitch at each edge without leaving a
hole: that's how the cardigan's sleeves widen.

## Yarn overs and decreases

- **Yarn over (yo)** in a forward pass: wrap the yarn over the hook and carry
  on. The wrap sits on the hook as a loop and is worked off in the return
  pass like any other. It adds a stitch and makes a hole. On the next row it
  has a front and back bar like a stitch, or you can work straight into the
  hole.
- **Tss2tog:** insert under the front bars of the **next two stitches at
  once**, yarn over, pull through both. One loop from two stitches: a
  decrease.
- Paired as **[tss2tog, yo]**, the count stays the same and you get a row of
  eyelets. That pair is the basis of the arrowhead stitch below.

## The arrowhead stitch

A two-row lace repeat over an even number of stitches. Row counts include the
foundation row, which is a closed row.

- **Open row:** [tss2tog, yo] across to the last stitch, Lts, return pass.
- **Closed row:** [tss 1, tks into the yo's hole] across to the last stitch,
  Lts, return pass.

The open row makes the holes, the closed row shuts them again, and the
decreases lean into little arrowheads pointing up the fabric. Patterns that
use it end on an open row and bind off "in pattern" (below), which does the
closed row and the bind-off in one go.

The commonest mistake is forgetting the yo after a tss2tog near the end of
the row. Count your loops before every return pass.

## Binding off

Like knitting, the last row has to be closed off, and the bind-off also gives
a clean, braided top edge to seam or add a border to.

**Slip-stitch bind-off:** skip the first bar as usual. Insert under the next
front vertical bar as for Tss, yarn over, and pull through the stitch **and**
the loop on the hook, a [slip stitch](../stitches/basic-stitches.md#slip-stitch-sl-st).
Repeat across. For the last stitch, go under both strands and slip stitch.
**Keep it loose**: a tight bind-off gathers the top edge and undoes all your
even tension. Then cut the yarn and pull it through
([fasten off](finishing.md#fastening-off)).

**Bind off in pattern:** work each stitch as the row's stitch pattern says
(tss, tks the yo, whatever it is), and after each one pull the new loop
through the loop on the hook. Used when the last row of the fabric needs to
close a lace row.

## Counting

- **Stitches:** count the loops on the hook at the end of every forward pass.
  It should equal the number of chains you started with, unless the row
  increases or decreases. Counting before the return pass is cheap; finding a
  dropped stitch three rows down is not.
- **Rows:** count the rows of vertical bars up the fabric. In a lace pattern,
  count open and closed rows: foundation (closed) is 1, the first open row
  is 2, and so on.

## Curling and tension

Tunisian fabric **curls towards you** along the bottom edge, more than
ordinary crochet does, because the work is never turned and all the pull sits
on the front side. The guide's remedies:

- **Loose tension and tall loops.** Pull up each loop to the full height of
  the row and don't yank the return pass tight.
- **A bigger hook** than the yarn's label suggests (see above).

And from the cardigan video and our own experience:

- **Block it.** Wet-blocking does the most for wool and other animal fibres;
  steam for acrylic. See [blocking](finishing.md#blocking) and
  [blocking a garment](garments.md#blocking-a-garment).
- A border or ribbing along the edge holds it flat, and the curl eases as
  the piece grows; the first few rows always look worst. (Ours.)

## Right side and wrong side

The side facing you as you work is the right side: vertical bars, and in the
arrowhead stitch the little arrows point up. The back shows the horizontal
ridges of the return passes. They look very different, so when seaming
pieces, check that every piece has its right side out and the arrows the same
way up. The designer admits she got it wrong the first time.

## Common mistakes

- **Working into the first vertical bar.** One stitch too many every row and
  a wavy right edge. The loop on the hook is stitch one; skip the bar under
  it.
- **Catching only one strand of the last stitch.** A loose, loopy left edge.
  Get the bar and its shadow.
- **Catching the back bar as well as the front.** Tss is the front bar only;
  pull the loop up and check it came from one strand.
- **Working into the row below.** The working row has a gap between its bars;
  the row below is filled in. Count rows if in doubt.
- **Starting the return pass with "yarn over, through two".** The row ends a
  stitch short and the right edge slopes. Always yarn over and pull through
  one first.
- **Foundation into the V's of the chain instead of the back bumps.** It
  works, but the bottom edge is untidy and harder to add ribbing to.
- **Tight return passes and bind-offs.** The fabric bunches and the top edge
  gathers.
- **Losing the yarn over** after a tss2tog in a lace row. The count drops by
  one and the pattern shifts.
- **Losing count on a long row.** Put a locking marker every 10 or 20
  stitches on a wide panel. (Ours.)

## Reading a Tunisian pattern

Tunisian patterns add a few abbreviations to the usual ones in
[reading patterns](../reading-patterns.md):

| Abbr.    | Meaning                                                        |
| -------- | -------------------------------------------------------------- |
| FwP      | forward pass                                                   |
| RetP     | return pass: ch 1, then [yo, pull through 2] until 1 loop left |
| Tss      | Tunisian simple stitch, under the front vertical bar           |
| Tks      | Tunisian knit stitch, between the front and back bars          |
| Tfs      | Tunisian full stitch, into the space between two stitches      |
| Tss2tog  | two stitches worked together as one Tss                        |
| Lts      | last Tunisian stitch, under both strands of the edge stitch    |
| yo       | yarn over, kept on the hook as a stitch                        |

Conventions that are assumed rather than written: the loop on the hook is
the first stitch and the first bar is skipped; the stitch count after a row
is the number of loops on the hook at the end of the forward pass; "RetP" at
the end of a row means the standard return pass; "bind off" means the
slip-stitch bind-off unless it says "in pattern".
